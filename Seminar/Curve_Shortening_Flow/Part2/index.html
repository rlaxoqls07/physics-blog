Evolution Equations

In the previous section, we derived that \(\partial_t ds = - \kappa^2 ds\) . This showed that the length of the curve decreases.

\[ \dot{L}(t) = \int_{\Gamma_t} -\kappa^2 ds \le 0 \]

2.1 Evolution of Geometric Quantities

The strictly decreasing nature of the curve's length provides a powerful geometric intuition: the flow acts like a perfectly elastic rubber band dissipating energy. If we constrain this rubber band by fixing its endpoints, it must continuously contract until it reaches the absolute minimum of the length functional for that boundary condition.

Corollary 2.1. Let \(\Gamma_0\) be a curve segment with two fixed endpoints. If \(\Gamma_t\) evolves by curve shortening flow subject to Dirichlet boundary conditions (the endpoints remain fixed for all \(t\) ), the curve asymptotically evolves into a straight line connecting the two points.

While it is geometrically intuitive that the curve shortening flow decreases total length, understanding the exact evolution of the curve's geometry requires us to track how differential operators interact along the flow. Because the arc-length parameter \(s\) depends on the time-evolving metric \(g\) , the time derivative \(\partial_t\) and the spatial derivative \(\partial_s\) do not commute. We must first establish their commutator relation to derive the evolution equations for the curve's intrinsic quantities.

Lemma 2.2.

\[ \left [ \partial_t , \partial_s \right ] = \kappa^2 \partial_s \]

That is,

\[ \partial_t \partial_s f - \partial_s \partial_t f = \kappa^2 \partial_s f. \]

Proof. We know that

\[ \begin{aligned} \partial_s f = \frac{1}{g} \frac{\partial f}{\partial u} \implies \partial_t \partial_s f &= \frac{1}{g} \frac{\partial ^2 f}{\partial t \partial u} - \frac{g_t}{g^2} \frac{\partial f}{\partial u} \\ &= \frac{1}{g} \frac{\partial ^2 f}{\partial u \partial t} + \kappa^2 \frac{\partial f}{\partial s} \end{aligned} \]

\(\blacksquare\)

For example, we compute \(\left ( T_s \right )_t\) .

Example 2.3. For example,

\[ \begin{aligned} T_t = \gamma_{st} &= \gamma_{ts} + \kappa ^2 \gamma_s \\ &= \left ( \kappa N \right )_s + \kappa ^2 T \\ &= \kappa_s N. \end{aligned} \]

Similarly,

\[ N_t = - \kappa_s T \]

holds. Also, we get \(\kappa_t\) from the following.

\[ \begin{aligned} \left ( T_s \right )_t &= T_{ts} + \kappa^2 T_s \\ &= \left ( \kappa_s N \right )_s + \kappa^3 N \\ &= \kappa_{ss} N - \kappa_s \kappa T + \kappa^3 N \end{aligned} \]

But, \((T_s)_t\) also satisfies

\[ (T_s)_t = (\kappa N)_t = \kappa_t N + \kappa N_t. \]

Then, by equating the normal terms, we get

\[ \kappa_t = \kappa_{ss} + \kappa^3. \]

2.2 The Maximum Principle and Convexity

The evolution equation \(\kappa_t = \kappa_{ss} + \kappa^3\) is a standard reaction-diffusion equation, revealing the competing forces within the flow. The diffusion term ( \(\kappa_{ss}\) ) acts to smooth out variations in curvature, while the nonlinear reaction term ( \(\kappa^3\) ) drives the curvature toward finite-time blow-up at highly curved regions. To rigorously analyze which force dominates and how the curvature is bounded, we rely on the fundamental tool for parabolic PDEs: the maximum principle.

Theorem 2.4 (Maximum principle). Suppose that \(\left ( \partial_t - \partial_{ss} \right ) u \le 0\) for \(u \in \mathcal{C}^{2,1} (S^1 \times [0,T])\) . Then,

\[ \max_{S^1} u(\cdot , t) \le \max_{S^1} u( \cdot , 0) \]

for all \(t \ge 0\) .

Proof. Let \(M = \max_{x \in S^1} u(x, 0)\) . We want to show that \(u(x,t) \le M\) for all \((x,t) \in S^1 \times [0,T]\) .

We proceed by contradiction. To avoid edge cases where spatial derivatives might vanish without yielding a strict sign, we introduce a small perturbation. For any \(\varepsilon > 0\) , define a new function

\[ u_\varepsilon (x,t) = u(x,t) - \varepsilon t. \]

Applying the heat operator to \(u_\varepsilon\) , we find

\[ \left ( \partial_t - \partial_{ss} \right ) u_\varepsilon = \left ( \partial_t - \partial_{ss} \right ) u - \varepsilon. \]

Since we are given that \((\partial_t - \partial_{ss}) u \le 0\) , it follows strictly that

\[ \left ( \partial_t - \partial_{ss} \right ) u_\varepsilon \le -\varepsilon < 0. \]

Now, assume for the sake of contradiction that the theorem is false. This means there is some point where \(u(x,t) > M\) . If so, for a sufficiently small \(\varepsilon > 0\) , there must also exist some point where \(u_\varepsilon(x,t) > M\) .

Since \(S^1 \times [0,T]\) is compact and \(u_\varepsilon(x,0) = u(x,0) \le M\) , there must exist a first time \(t_0 > 0\) and a spatial point \(x_0 \in S^1\) such that

\[ u_\varepsilon(x_0, t_0) = M \quad \text{and} \quad u_\varepsilon(x, t) \le M \text{ for all } x \in S^1, \, t \le t_0. \]

At this point \((x_0, t_0)\) , the function \(u_\varepsilon\) attains a local spatial maximum, which implies two necessary conditions from elementary calculus

\[ \partial_s u_\varepsilon(x_0, t_0) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \partial_{ss} u_\varepsilon(x_0, t_0) \le 0. \]

Furthermore, because \(t_0\) is the very first time the function reaches the value \(M\) (it was strictly less than or equal to \(M\) for all \(t < t_0\) ), the function must be non-decreasing in time at that instant

\[ \partial_t u_\varepsilon(x_0, t_0) \ge 0. \]

Combining these calculus facts, we evaluate the heat operator at \((x_0, t_0)\)

\[ \left ( \partial_t - \partial_{ss} \right ) u_\varepsilon(x_0, t_0) \ge 0 - 0 = 0. \]

However, this directly contradicts our earlier strict derivation that \((\partial_t - \partial_{ss}) u_\varepsilon < 0\) everywhere on the domain.

Because of this contradiction, our assumption must be false. Therefore, \(u_\varepsilon(x,t) \le M\) for all \((x,t)\) . Taking the limit as \(\varepsilon \to 0\) yields

\[ u(x,t) \le M = \max_{x \in S^1} u(x, 0), \]

which completes the proof. \(\blacksquare\)

Now we give a corollary about time evolution of bound of curvature.

Corollary 2.5. Suppose that the curvature is bounded by

\[ \kappa(\cdot , 0) \ge m_0 > 0. \]

Then, for \(t > 0\) , \(\kappa(\cdot , t) \ge m_0\) . Moreover,

\[ \kappa(\cdot , t) \ge \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1-2m_0 ^2 t}} \]

where the (RHS) is the solution to the ODE \(\dot{f} = f^3\) with \(f(0) = m_0\) .

Proof.

Step 0 : Skeleton of Proof We prove by using the maximum principle. We would first prove the following statement, which implies our corollary.

Step 1 : General Statement Suppose that there are two functions \(f_1 ( u,t)\) and \(f_2 (u,t)\) satisfying

\[ (f_1)_t - (f_1)_{ss} = f_1 ^3 , \quad (f_2)_t - (f_2)_{ss} < f_2 ^3 \]

and \(f_1 > f_2\) at \(t=0\) . Then, \(f_1 \ge f_2\) for \(t \ge 0\) , \(u \in S^1\) .

Step 2 : Proof of General Statement We prove the statement above. Suppose not, that is, there exist a first time \((t_0 , u_0)\) such that \(f_1 = f_2\) . At this point,

\[ f_1 = f_2, \quad (f_1 - f_2)_t\le 0, \quad (f_1 - f_2)_{ss} \ge 0. \]

Then,

\[ 0 = (f_1)_t - (f_1)_{ss} - f_1 ^3 \le (f_2)_t - (f_2)_{ss} - f_2 ^3 < 0 \]

which gives a contradiction.

Step 3 : Implication to Corollary Let \(f_1(s,t) = \kappa(s,t)\) . From our earlier derivation, the evolution equation for curvature under curve shortening flow is \(\kappa_t = \kappa_{ss} + \kappa^3\) . Therefore, \(f_1\) exactly satisfies the parabolic equation \((f_1)_t - (f_1)_{ss} = f_1^3\) .

To apply the strict inequality from Step 1, we introduce a small constant \(\varepsilon > 0\) and define \(f_2^\varepsilon(t)\) as the spatially independent solution to the perturbed ODE

\[ \frac{d}{dt} f_2^\varepsilon(t) = (f_2^\varepsilon(t))^3 - \varepsilon \]

with the initial condition \(f_2^\varepsilon(0) = m_0 - \varepsilon\) .

Since \(f_2^\varepsilon\) is independent of the spatial parameter \(s\) , we have \((f_2^\varepsilon)_{ss} = 0\) . Thus, it satisfies the strict subsolution condition

\[ (f_2^\varepsilon)_t - (f_2^\varepsilon)_{ss} = (f_2^\varepsilon)^3 - \varepsilon < (f_2^\varepsilon)^3. \]

At the initial time \(t=0\) , we are given \(\kappa(\cdot, 0) \ge m_0\) . Therefore, we have a strict ordering of the initial data

\[ f_1(\cdot, 0) = \kappa(\cdot, 0) \ge m_0 > m_0 - \varepsilon = f_2^\varepsilon(0). \]

Since all the hypotheses of Step 1 are satisfied, we conclude that \(f_1(\cdot, t) \ge f_2^\varepsilon(t)\) for all valid \(t \ge 0\) .

Now, by taking the limit as \(\varepsilon \to 0\) , the function \(f_2^\varepsilon(t)\) converges smoothly to \(f(t)\) , where \(f(t)\) is the exact solution to \(\dot{f} = f^3\) with \(f(0) = m_0\) . Solving this separable ODE yields

\[ f(t) = \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - 2m_0^2 t}}. \]

Thus, the limit gives us our primary bound

\[ \kappa(\cdot, t) \ge \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - 2m_0^2 t}}. \]

Finally, since \(t \ge 0\) , we know that \(\sqrt{1 - 2m_0^2 t} \le 1\) . This implies that \(f(t) \ge m_0\) , which immediately guarantees \(\kappa(\cdot, t) \ge m_0\) . This completes the proof. \(\blacksquare\)

The previous bound strictly applies to curves with strictly positive initial curvature. However, by taking the limit as the initial minimum curvature \(m_0 \searrow 0\) , we immediately obtain one of the most fundamental shape-preserving properties of the curve shortening flow: convexity is preserved.

Corollary 2.6. If a curve is convex, i.e., \(\kappa \ge 0\) for \(t=0\) , then the curve is convex for \(t > 0\) , that is, \(\kappa \ge 0\) for \(t > 0\) .

2.3 Short-Time Existence

We have established that convex curves remain convex and that curvature cannot jump from positive to negative. However, this does not guarantee the flow exists indefinitely. The nonlinear nature of the curvature evolution strongly suggests that the geometry will eventually collapse. To formalize this, we must first establish that a unique solution exists, at least for a short time, and then identify the precise mechanism that forces the flow to terminate.

Theorem 2.7 (Short time existence). Let \(\Gamma_0\) be a \(\mathcal{C}^\infty\) simple closed curve. Then, the curve shortening flow \(\Gamma_t\) exists for a short time \(t \in [0,T)\) for \(T < \infty\) . Here, \(\Gamma_t\) is smooth, simple, closed, and the solution is unique. Moreover, if \(T\) is the maximal time of existence, then

\[ \max_{\Gamma_t} \kappa \nearrow \infty \quad \text{as} \quad t \nearrow T. \]

How far can we expand the curve shortening flow?

Theorem 2.8. Suppose that \(\Gamma_t\) is a curve shortening flow for \(t\in [0,T)\) and \(\sup_{\Gamma_t} |\kappa| < C < \infty\) for \(0 \le t \le T\) . Then, \(\Gamma_t\) can be extended to \(t\in [0,T+\varepsilon)\) .

The extension theorem dictates that the flow can only terminate if the curvature itself blows up. However, merely bounding the scalar curvature is not obviously sufficient to guarantee that the curve's higher-order geometry doesn't develop microscopic kinks. To seamlessly extend the flow, we must ensure that all geometric derivatives remain perfectly controlled. Because curve shortening flow is a parabolic system, it instantly smooths out rough initial data, yielding the following Bernstein-Bando-Shi type estimates.

Theorem 2.9. Suppose that \(|\kappa| \le A <\infty\) for \(t \in [0,T]\) . Then,

\[ \left \vert \partial_s ^m \kappa \right \vert \le C \cdot \frac{1}{t^{m/2}} \]

for some \(C = C(A,T) < \infty\) .

Proof.

Step 1 : \(m=1\) We have

\[ \begin{aligned} \kappa_t &= \kappa_{ss} + \kappa^3 \\ \kappa_{ts} &= \kappa_{sss} + 3 \kappa^2 \kappa_s \\ \kappa_{st} &= \kappa_{sss} + 3 \kappa^2 \kappa_s + \kappa^2 \kappa_s \\ &= \kappa_{sss} + 4 \kappa^2 \kappa_s. \end{aligned} \]

So, we have that \(\partial_t \kappa_s - \partial_{ss} \kappa_s = 4 \kappa^2 \kappa_s\) . Now we calculate the following for a reason that is used after.

\[ \begin{aligned} \partial_t (\kappa_s )^2 - \partial_{ss} (\kappa_s )^2 &= 2 \kappa_s (4\kappa^2 \kappa_s) - 2 \kappa_{ss}^2 \\ &= 8 \kappa^2 \kappa_s ^2 - 2 \kappa_{ss}^2 \end{aligned} \]

Also,

\[ \partial_t \kappa^2 - \partial_{ss} \kappa^2 = 2 \kappa^4 - 2 \kappa_s ^2. \]

Consider some function \(f = t \kappa_s ^2 + \beta \kappa^2\) for \(0 < \beta < \infty\) . By the computation above,

\[ f_t - f_{ss} = 8t \kappa^2 \kappa_s ^2 - t \kappa_{ss}^2 + \kappa_s ^2 + \beta \left (2\kappa^4 - 2 \kappa_s ^2 \right ) \]

By choosing \(\beta\) large enough, this is bounded by some constant \(2 \beta A^4\) . Also, suppose that \(f(0) \le \beta A^2\) . By the maximal principle, \(f \le g\) where \(g\) solves

\[ \dot{g} = 2 \beta A^4 , \quad g(0) = \beta A^2, \]

which gives \(f \le C\) . Then, this also implies that \(t \kappa_s ^2 \le C\) , and thus

\[ |\kappa_s| \le \frac{1}{\sqrt{t}} C. \]

Step 2 : \(m > 1\) For \(m>1\) , we can set an appropriate \(f\) , and bound it by \(C \cdot 1/t^{m/2}\) . \(\blacksquare\)

Precise proof for part 2 requires advanced knowledge in PDE.

Remark. We can now explicitly see how Theorem 2.9 implies the extension in Theorem 2.8. If the magnitude of the curvature \(|\kappa|\) remains uniformly bounded as \(t \to T\) , the derivative estimates guarantee that all higher-order spatial derivatives of the curvature are also uniformly bounded. This translates to uniform bounds on all derivatives of the position vector \(\gamma\) . By providing both uniform boundedness and equicontinuity, these bounds satisfy the exact conditions needed for the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem. Thus, we can extract a smoothly converging limit, proving that \(\Gamma_t \to \Gamma_T\) as a perfectly well-defined \(\mathcal{C}^\infty\) curve. Because \(\Gamma_T\) is smooth, we can simply apply the short-time existence theorem using \(\Gamma_T\) as our new initial data, thereby extending the flow seamlessly into \([0, T+\varepsilon)\) .

Because all higher-order derivatives are controlled solely by the maximum of the curvature, the geometry cannot degenerate in wild, unpredictable ways. This strict control over the curve's regularity forces a stark conclusion: the flow must eventually encounter a definitive singularity where the maximum curvature strictly diverges to infinity.

Corollary 2.10. Let \(\Gamma_0\) be a \(\mathcal{C}^\infty\) simple closed curve. Then, there exists a unique \(T< \infty\) such that

\[ \sup_{t<T} \sup_{\Gamma_t} \kappa = \infty. \]

Here, \(T\) is called the singular time .

2.4 Rescaling and Singularity Formation

By Theorem 2.8 and the definition of the singular time \(T\) , we know that the flow develops a singularity precisely because the curvature blows up to infinity. To understand the geometric shape of the curve as it collapses, we must "zoom in" on the singularity both in space and in time. Because the curve shortening flow is governed by a heat-type equation, the natural way to blow up the singularity is via parabolic rescaling, which preserves the structure of the flow equation.

Lemma 2.11. Let \(\Gamma_t\) be a curve shortening flow. Then, for \(\Lambda > 0\) , \(\tilde{\Gamma}_t = \Lambda \Gamma_{t/\Lambda^2}\) is also a curve shortening flow.

Proof. Let \(\tilde{\gamma}(u,t) = \Lambda \gamma(u, t/\Lambda^2)\) . Taking the time derivative yields

\[ \partial_t \tilde{\gamma} = \Lambda \cdot \frac{1}{\Lambda^2} \partial_\tau \gamma = \frac{1}{\Lambda} \vec{K} \]

where \(\tau = t/\Lambda^2\) . For the scaled curve, the normal vector is unchanged ( \(\tilde{N} = N\) ), but the spatial derivatives scale inversely, meaning the new curvature is \(\tilde{\kappa} = \frac{1}{\Lambda} \kappa\) . Therefore, the new curvature vector is \(\vec{\tilde{K}} = \tilde{\kappa} \tilde{N} = \frac{1}{\Lambda} \vec{K}\) .

Substituting this back, we get \(\partial_t \tilde{\gamma} = \vec{\tilde{K}}\) , which perfectly satisfies the curve shortening flow equation. \(\blacksquare\)

Now we look for some monotone quantities along the curve shortening flow. We know that

\[ \dot{L} = - \int_{\Gamma_t} \kappa^2 ds \le 0 \]

is monotone. However, this is not a good quantity since it varies by rescaling.

There exists some good monotone quantity, namely weighted length . When studying how curves shrink near singularities, the weighted length is usually used.

For given \(X_0 = (x_0 , t_0)\) as the spacetime point of the singularity, the weight, specifically backward heat kernel is given by

\[ \rho_{X_0}(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi (t_0 - t)}} e^{- \frac{|x-x_0|^2}{4(t_0 - t)}} \]

for \(t < t_0\) , \(x \in \mathbb{R}^2\) . We define the weighted length as

\[ \Phi_{X_0} (t) = \int_{\Gamma_t} \rho_{X_0} (x , t) ds(x). \]

We can check that \(\Phi_{X_0}\) is rescaling invariant.

Remark. To verify that the weighted length \(\Phi_{X_0}(t)\) is invariant under parabolic rescaling, we perform a change of variables centered at the singularity \(X_0 = (x_0, t_0)\) .

Define the rescaled space and time coordinates as

\[ \tilde{x} = \Lambda(x-x_0), \quad \tau = \Lambda^2 (t-t_0) \]

where \(\Lambda > 0\) . Under this transformation, the singular point \(X_0\) is mapped to the origin in the new coordinates, so our new base point is \(\tilde{X}_0 = (0,0)\) .

First, we calculate how the backward heat kernel \(\rho\) transforms. In the rescaled coordinates, the weight function evaluated around the new origin is

\[ \rho_{(0,0)}(\tilde{x}, \tau) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi (-\tau)}} \exp\left({- \frac{|\tilde{x}|^2}{4(-\tau)}}\right). \]

Substituting our definitions for \(\tilde{x}\) and \(\tau\) into this expression yields

\[ \begin{aligned} \rho_{(0,0)}(\tilde{x}, \tau) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi (-\Lambda^2(t-t_0))}} \exp\left({- \frac{|\Lambda(x-x_0)|^2}{4(-\Lambda^2(t-t_0))}}\right) \\ &= \frac{1}{\Lambda \sqrt{4\pi (t_0-t)}} \exp\left({- \frac{\Lambda^2|x-x_0|^2}{4\Lambda^2(t_0-t)}}\right) \\ &= \frac{1}{\Lambda} \rho_{X_0}(x, t). \end{aligned} \]

Next, we consider the arc length measure. Because the rescaled curve \(\tilde{\Gamma}_\tau\) is a pure spatial dilation of \(\Gamma_t\) (shifted by \(x_0\) ) by a factor of \(\Lambda\) , the 1-dimensional arc length element scales linearly

\[ d\tilde{s}(\tilde{x}) = \Lambda \, ds(x). \]

Finally, we evaluate the total weighted length of the rescaled curve

\[ \begin{aligned} \Phi_{(0,0)}(\tau) &= \int_{\tilde{\Gamma}_\tau} \rho_{(0,0)}(\tilde{x}, \tau) d\tilde{s}(\tilde{x}) \\ &= \int_{\Gamma_t} \left( \frac{1}{\Lambda} \rho_{X_0}(x, t) \right) \big( \Lambda \, ds(x) \big) \\ &= \int_{\Gamma_t} \rho_{X_0}(x, t) ds(x) \\ &= \Phi_{X_0}(t). \end{aligned} \]

This precise cancelation of the scaling factor \(\Lambda\) demonstrates that the weighted length functional is perfectly invariant under parabolic rescaling.

Now we introduce a theorem that shows the monotonicity of the weighted length \(\Phi_{X_0}\) .

Theorem 2.12 (Huisken) 1 .

\[ \frac{d}{dt} \Phi_{X_0} (t) = - \int_{\Gamma_t} \left( \kappa + \frac{\langle x - x_0 , N \rangle}{2 (t_0 - t)} \right)^2 \rho_{X_0} ds \le 0. \]

Proof. The time derivative of the weighted length functional is given by

\[ \frac{d}{dt} \Phi_{X_0}(t) = \frac{d}{dt} \int_{\Gamma_t} \rho_{X_0} ds = \int_{\Gamma_t} \left( \frac{d}{dt} \rho_{X_0} \right) ds + \int_{\Gamma_t} \rho_{X_0} (\partial_t ds). \]

From our earlier evolution equations, we know that the arc length measure evolves as \(\partial_t ds = -\kappa^2 ds\) . By the chain rule, the total time derivative of the weight \(\rho_{X_0}(x(t), t)\) along the flow \(\partial_t x = \kappa N\) is

\[ \frac{d}{dt} \rho_{X_0} = \partial_t \rho_{X_0} + \langle \nabla \rho_{X_0} , \partial_t x \rangle = \partial_t \rho_{X_0} + \kappa \langle \nabla \rho_{X_0} , N \rangle. \]

Direct differentiation of the backward heat kernel \(\rho_{X_0}(x,t) = (4\pi(t_0-t))^{-1/2} \exp\left(-\frac{|x-x_0|^2}{4(t_0-t)}\right)\) yields

\[ \partial_t \rho_{X_0} = \rho_{X_0} \left[ \frac{1}{2(t_0 - t)} - \frac{|x-x_0|^2}{4(t_0 - t)^2} \right], \quad \nabla \rho_{X_0} = - \rho_{X_0} \frac{x-x_0}{2(t_0 - t)}. \]

Substituting these into our integral, we get the raw evolution equation

\[ \frac{d}{dt} \Phi_{X_0}(t) = \int_{\Gamma_t} \rho_{X_0} \left[ \frac{1}{2(t_0 - t)} - \frac{|x-x_0|^2}{4(t_0 - t)^2} - \kappa \frac{\langle x-x_0, N \rangle}{2(t_0 - t)} - \kappa^2 \right] ds. \tag{2.1} \]

To complete the square, we use integration by parts on the spatial derivative along the curve. The tangential derivative of \(\rho_{X_0}\) is

\[ \partial_s \rho_{X_0} = \langle \nabla \rho_{X_0}, T \rangle = - \rho_{X_0} \frac{\langle x-x_0, T \rangle}{2(t_0 - t)}. \]

Because \(\Gamma_t\) is a closed curve, the integral of any exact spatial derivative vanishes. We consider the integral of \(\partial_s \left( \rho_{X_0} \frac{\langle x-x_0, T \rangle}{2(t_0-t)} \right)\)

\[ 0 = \int_{\Gamma_t} \partial_s \left( \rho_{X_0} \frac{\langle x-x_0, T \rangle}{2(t_0 - t)} \right) ds. \]

Applying the product rule and using \(\partial_s (x-x_0) = T\) and the Frenet-Serret formula \(\partial_s T = \kappa N\) , we get

\[ 0 = \int_{\Gamma_t} \left[ \partial_s \rho_{X_0} \frac{\langle x-x_0, T \rangle}{2(t_0 - t)} + \rho_{X_0} \frac{\langle T, T \rangle + \langle x-x_0, \kappa N \rangle}{2(t_0 - t)} \right] ds. \]

Substituting our identity for \(\partial_s \rho_{X_0}\) and using \(\langle T, T \rangle = 1\) , this becomes

\[ 0 = \int_{\Gamma_t} \rho_{X_0} \left[ - \frac{\langle x-x_0, T \rangle^2}{4(t_0 - t)^2} + \frac{1}{2(t_0 - t)} + \frac{\kappa \langle x-x_0, N \rangle}{2(t_0 - t)} \right] ds. \]

We now use the geometric decomposition \(|x-x_0|^2 = \langle x-x_0, T \rangle^2 + \langle x-x_0, N \rangle^2\) and subtract this zero-valued integral from our raw evolution equation (2.1).

The \(\frac{1}{2(t_0-t)}\) terms cancel completely. Furthermore, the tangential components \(\frac{\langle x-x_0, T \rangle^2}{4(t_0-t)^2}\) cancel out perfectly, leaving only the normal components and the curvature terms

\[ \frac{d}{dt} \Phi_{X_0}(t) = \int_{\Gamma_t} \rho_{X_0} \left[ - \frac{\langle x-x_0, N \rangle^2}{4(t_0 - t)^2} - \frac{2\kappa\langle x-x_0, N \rangle}{2(t_0 - t)} - \kappa^2 \right] ds. \]

Factoring out the negative sign completes the perfect square

\[ \frac{d}{dt} \Phi_{X_0}(t) = - \int_{\Gamma_t} \left( \kappa + \frac{\langle x - x_0 , N \rangle}{2 (t_0 - t)} \right)^2 \rho_{X_0} ds. \]

Since the backward heat kernel \(\rho_{X_0}\) is strictly positive and the squared term is non-negative, the entire integrand is non-negative. Thus, the time derivative is strictly \(\le 0\) , which completes the proof. \(\blacksquare\)

Notice that this monotonicity is strict unless the term inside the square vanishes identically.

Remark. The equality case in Huisken's theorem precisely characterizes the self-similar shrinkers of the flow. By the Abresch-Langer classification theorem (1986), the only embedded self-shrinkers in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) are the straight line through the origin and the circle. Thus, any singularity modeled by an embedded shrinker must be asymptotically round.
