    Manifolds

    1. Why Manifolds?

    Our experience tells us that spacetime is a four-dimensional continuum in the sense that it requires four numbers to characterize an event. All events in spacetime is assumed that it can be put into one-to-one continuous correspondence with the points of \(\mathbb{R}^4\).

    However, in general relativity, we will be solving for the spacetime geometry and we do not wish to prejudice in advance any aspects of global nature of spacetime structure.

    Before defining the notion of a manifold, we recall the definition of an open ball.

    An open ball in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) of radius \(r\) centered around point \(y = (y^1 , \cdots, y^n)\) consists of the points \(x\) such that \(|x-y| < r\), where $$ |x-y| = \left [ \sum_{\mu=1}^n (x^\mu - y^\mu )^2 \right ]. $$ An open set in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is any set which can be expressed as a union of open balls.

    2. Manifolds

    A manifold is a set made up of pieces that look like open subsets of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) such that these pieces can be sewn together smoothly. More precisely, an \(n\)-dimensional, \(\mathcal{C}^\infty\), real manifold \(M\) is a set together with a collection of subsets \(\{O_\alpha\}\) satisfying the following properties. 1. Each \(p\in M\) lies in at least one \(O_\alpha\), i.e., the collection \(\{O_\alpha\}\) covers \(M\). 2. For each \(\alpha\), there is a one-to-one, onto, map \(\psi_\alpha : O_\alpha \to U_\alpha\), where \(U_\alpha\) is an open subset of \(\mathbb{R}^n\). 3. If any two sets \(O_\alpha\) and \(O_\beta\) overlap, \(O_\alpha \cap O_\beta \ne \emptyset\), we can consider the map \(\psi_\beta \circ \psi_\alpha ^{-1}\) which takes points in \(\psi (O_\alpha \cap O_\beta)\) to points in \(\psi_\beta (O_\alpha \cap O_\beta)\). We require these subsets of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) to be open and this map to be \(\mathcal{C}^\infty\).

    Each map \(\psi_\alpha\) is called a chart by mathematicians or coordinate system by physicists. For convenience, we require that the cover \(\{O_\alpha \}\) and chart family \(\{\psi_\alpha\}\) to be maximal.

    Here, we consider manifolds which are Hausdorff and paracompact.

    3. Product Manifold

    Given two manifolds \(M\) and \(M'\) of dimension \(n\) and \(n'\) respectively, we can make the product space \(M \times M'\) into an \((n+n')\)-dimensional manifold as follows. If \(\psi_\alpha : O_\alpha \to U_\alpha\) and \(\psi_\beta ' : O_\beta ' \to U_\beta '\) are charts, we define \(U_{\alpha \beta} : O_{\alpha \beta} \to U_{\alpha \beta} \subset \mathbb{R}^{n+n'}\) on \(M \times M'\) by taking \(O_{\alpha \beta} = O_\alpha \times O_\beta '\) and \(U_{\alpha \beta} = U_\alpha \times U_\beta '\) and setting \(\psi_{\alpha \beta} (p,p') = \left [ \psi_\alpha (p) , \psi_\beta ' (p') \right ]\).

    4. Diffeomorphisms

    We may now define the notion of differentiability and smoothness of maps between manifolds.

    Let \(M\) and \(M'\) be manifolds and let \(\{\psi_\alpha\}\) and \(\{\psi'_\beta\}\) denote the chart maps. A map \(f:M \to M'\) is said to be \(\mathcal{C}^\infty\) if for each \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), the map \(\psi'_\beta \circ f \circ \psi_\alpha^{-1}\) taking \(U_\alpha \subset \mathbb{R}^n\) into \(U'_\beta \subset \mathbb{R}^{n'}\) is \(\mathcal{C}^\infty\) in the sense used in advanced calculus.

    If \(f:M \to M'\) is \(\mathcal{C}^\infty\), one-to-one, onto, and has \(\mathcal{C}^\infty\) inverse, \(f\) is called a diffeomorphism and \(M\) and \(M'\) are said to be diffeomorphic. Diffeomorphic manifolds have identical manifold structure.
