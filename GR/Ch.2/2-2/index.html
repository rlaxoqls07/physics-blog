2.2 Vectors

1. Vectors in Curved Geometry

In pre-relativistic physics, it is assumed that space has the natural structure of a 3-dimensional vector space, once one has designated a point to serve as an origin. In special relativity, Minkowski spacetime similarly has the natural structure of a 4-dimensional vector space.

However, when one considers curved geometry, the global vector space structure is lost. There is no natural or unique notion of how to add two points on a sphere and end up on the sphere. Nevertheless, the vector space structure can be recovered locally in the limit of infinitesimal displacements about a point.

We would like to give a rigorous mathematical definition of a tangent vector at \(p\) which only refers to the intrinsic structure of the manifold \(\mathcal{M}\) , completely independent of any embeddings in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) .

In \(\mathbb{R}^n\) , a standard vector \(v = (v^1, \dots, v^n)\) intrinsically defines the directional derivative operator \(\sum_\mu v^\mu \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu} \right)\) , and vice versa. Directional derivatives are entirely characterized by two algebraic properties: linearity and the Leibniz rule . We use this to abstract the concept of vectors.

Definition 2.2.1 (Tangent Vector). On a manifold \(\mathcal{M}\) , let \(\mathcal{F}\) denote the collection of \(\mathcal{C}^\infty\) functions from \(\mathcal{M}\) into \(\mathbb{R}\) . We define a tangent vector \(v\) at \(p \in \mathcal{M}\) to be a mapping \(v: \mathcal{F} \to \mathbb{R}\) which satisfies

  • Linearity: \(v(af + bg) = a v(f) + b v(g) \quad \forall f,g \in \mathcal{F}, \ a,b \in \mathbb{R}\) .
  • Leibniz rule : \(v(fg) = f(p)v(g) + g(p)v(f)\) .

If \(h \in \mathcal{F}\) is a constant function, i.e., \(h(q) = c \quad \forall q \in \mathcal{M}\) , then \(v(h) = 0\) because \(v(h^2) = 2cv(h)\) by the Leibniz rule , while \(v(h^2) = v(ch) = cv(h)\) by linearity. This forces \(v(h)=0\) . This mathematical behavior gives a precise meaning to the concept of an infinitesimal displacement (it cannot alter a constant value).

Let \(V_p\) be the collection of all tangent vectors at \(p\) . We can define addition and scalar multiplication naturally: \((v_1 + v_2)(f) = v_1(f) + v_2(f)\) and \((av)(f) = a v(f)\) , meaning \(V_p\) forms a vector space.

Theorem 2.2.1 (Dimension of Tangent Space). > Let \(\mathcal{M}\) be an \(n\) -dimensional manifold. Let \(p \in \mathcal{M}\) and let \(V_p\) denote the tangent space at \(p\) . Then, \(\dim V_p = n\) .

Proof. > We shall show that \(\dim V_p = n\) by constructing a basis of \(V_p\) , i.e., by finding \(n\) linearly independent tangent vectors which span \(V_p\) .

Let \(\psi: O \to U \subset \mathbb{R}^n\) be a chart with \(p \in O\) . For \(\mu = 1, \dots, n\) , define an operator \(X_\mu: \mathcal{F} \to \mathbb{R}\) by evaluating the partial derivative in the local coordinate patch

\[X_\mu(f) = \left. \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu} (f \circ \psi^{-1}) \right|_{\psi(p)}\]

where \((x^1, \dots, x^n)\) are the Cartesian coordinates of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) . It is easily verified that \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are linearly independent tangent vectors.

To show that they span \(V_p\) , we expand \(f \circ \psi^{-1}\) around \(a = \psi(p)\) using Taylor's theorem. For all \(q \in O\) we have

\[f(q) = f(p) + \sum_{\mu=1}^n [x^\mu \circ \psi(q) - x^\mu \circ \psi(p)] H_\mu(\psi(q)).\]

where the smooth functions \(H_\mu\) satisfy \(H_\mu(\psi(p)) = X_\mu(f)\) .

Let \(v \in V_p\) be an arbitrary tangent vector. We apply \(v\) to \(f\) , using linearity, the Leibniz property, and the fact that \(v\) applied to a constant vanishes. Evaluating at \(p\) , the terms where \(v\) acts on \(H_\mu\) zero out because the position difference \([x^\mu \circ \psi(p) - x^\mu \circ \psi(p)]\) is zero at \(p\) . We obtain

\[v(f) = \sum_{\mu=1}^n [H_\mu \circ \psi(p)] v(x^\mu \circ \psi).\]

Substituting \(H_\mu \circ \psi(p) = X_\mu(f)\) , we get

\[v(f) = \sum_{\mu=1}^n v^\mu X_\mu(f)\]

where the coefficients are defined as \(v^\mu = v(x^\mu \circ \psi)\) .

Thus, we have successfully expressed an arbitrary tangent vector \(v\) as a linear combination of the \(X_\mu\)

\[v = \sum_{\mu=1}^n v^\mu X_\mu.\]

This shows the \(n\) vectors \(X_\mu\) span the tangent space, completing the proof that \(\dim V_p = n\) . \(\blacksquare\)

The basis \(\{X_\mu \}\) constructed in the proof is called a coordinate basis . We often denote \(X_\mu\) simply as \(\partial / \partial x^\mu\) .

2. Basis Transformation and Vector Transformation

By choosing a different chart \(\psi'\) , we obtain a different coordinate basis \(\{X_\mu'\}\) . Since \(\{X_\mu'\}\) also spans \(V_p\) , we can express \(X_\mu\) in terms of the new basis \(\{X_\mu'\}\) . Using the multivariable chain rule, we get

\[ X_{\mu} = \sum_{\nu = 1}^n \left. \frac{\partial x'^\nu}{\partial x^\mu} \right|_{\psi(p)} X_{\nu}' \]

where \(x'^\nu\) denotes the \(\nu\) -th component of the transition map \(\psi' \circ \psi^{-1}\) . Hence, equating \(v = \sum v^\mu X_\mu = \sum v'^\nu X_\nu'\) , we find that components \(v'^\nu\) of a vector \(v\) in the new coordinate basis are related to the components \(v^\mu\) in the old basis by

\[ v'^\nu = \sum_{\mu=1}^n v^\mu \frac{\partial x'^\nu}{\partial x^\mu} \]

This is universally known as the vector transformation law .

3. Tangent of a Curve

A smooth curve \(C\) on a manifold \(\mathcal{M}\) is a \(\mathcal{C}^\infty\) map from \(\mathbb{R}\) into \(\mathcal{M}\) (often parameterized by \(t\) or \(\lambda\) ).

At each point \(p \in \mathcal{M}\) lying on the curve, we can associate a tangent vector \(T \in V_p\) with \(C\) as follows. For any \(f \in \mathcal{F}\) , set \(T(f) = d(f \circ C)/dt\) . Using the chain rule,

\[ T(f) = \frac{d}{dt} (f \circ C) = \sum_{\mu=1}^n \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu} (f \circ \psi^{-1}) \frac{dx^\mu}{dt} = \sum_{\mu=1}^n \frac{dx^\mu}{dt} X_{\mu} (f) \]

because the curve \(C\) on \(\mathcal{M}\) gets mapped into a coordinate curve \(x^\mu(t)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) when we choose a coordinate system \(\psi\) . Thus, the components \(T^\mu\) of the tangent vector to the curve are simply the velocity components.

\[ T^\mu = \frac{dx^\mu}{dt} \]

Remark (The Parallel Transport Problem). We can define another tangent space \(V_q\) by choosing a different point \(q \in \mathcal{M}\) . However, we do not have a natural way of identifying \(V_p\) and \(V_q\) ; there is no absolute way of determining whether a tangent vector at \(q\) is "the same" as a tangent vector at \(p\) .

If we are given an additional structure called a connection (e.g., the Christoffel symbols), we gain a notion of parallel transport of vectors from \(p\) to \(q\) along a curve. However, if the spacetime curvature is nonzero, the identification of \(V_p\) with \(V_q\) will strictly depend on the path chosen.

A tangent field (or vector field) \(v\) on a manifold \(\mathcal{M}\) is an assignment of a tangent vector \(v|_p \in V_p\) at each point \(p \in \mathcal{M}\) . Even though we do not have a notion of whether \(V_p\) and \(V_q\) are the same, we have a natural notion of \(v\) being smooth. If \(f\) is a \(\mathcal{C}^\infty\) function, then at each \(p \in \mathcal{M}\) , \(v|_p(f)\) is a number, making \(v(f)\) a scalar function on \(\mathcal{M}\) . The vector field \(v\) is smooth if for each smooth function \(f\) , the resulting function \(v(f)\) is also smooth. In other words, a vector field \(v\) is smooth if and only if its coordinate basis components \(v^\mu\) are smooth functions of the coordinates.

We discussed that the vector space structure can be recovered in the limit of infinitesimal displacements about a point. We now show a precise meaning of this idea.

4. Vector Fields, Orbits, and Commutators

Definition 2.2.2 (One-Parameter Group of Diffeomorphisms). A one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms \(\phi_t\) is a \(\mathcal{C}^\infty\) map from \(\mathbb{R} \times \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{M}\) such that for a fixed \(t \in \mathbb{R}\) , \(\phi_t : \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{M}\) is a diffeomorphism, and for all \(t,s \in \mathbb{R}\) , we have the composition property \(\phi_t \circ \phi_s = \phi_{t+s}\) .

For a fixed \(p \in \mathcal{M}\) , \(\phi_t(p) : \mathbb{R} \to \mathcal{M}\) is a curve, called an orbit of \(\phi_t\) , which passes through \(p\) at \(t=0\) . We can define \(v|_p\) to be the tangent to this curve at \(t=0\) . Associated to a one-parameter group of transformations of \(\mathcal{M}\) is a vector field \(v\) , which can conceptually be thought of as the infinitesimal generator of these global transformations.

Conversely, given a smooth vector field \(v\) on \(\mathcal{M}\) , we can ask if it is possible to find integral curves of \(v\) . An integral curve is a curve in \(\mathcal{M}\) having the property that one and only one curve passes through each point \(p \in \mathcal{M}\) , and the tangent to this curve at \(p\) is exactly \(v|_p\) .

Yes, we can. If we pick a coordinate system in a neighborhood of \(p\) as in the previous proof, we see that the problem of finding such curves reduces to solving the following system of ordinary differential equations in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) .

\[ \frac{d x^\mu}{dt} = v^\mu (x^1, \dots, x^n) \]

By standard mathematical theorems, this system of equations has a unique solution for smooth \(v^\mu\) , and thus every smooth vector field yields a unique non-intersecting family of integral curves (representing the "flow" of the vector field).

Since vector fields are essentially differential operators, they do not generally commute when applied to functions.

Definition 2.2.3 ( Commutator / Lie Bracket). Given two vector fields \(v\) and \(w\) , we can define a new vector field \([v,w]\) .

\[[v,w](f) = v[w(f)] - w[v(f)]\]

This is called the commutator (or Lie bracket), by its action on a function \(f\) .

Note that the commutator of any two coordinate basis vector fields \(X_\mu = \partial/\partial x^\mu\) and \(X_\nu = \partial/\partial x^\nu\) identically vanishes due to the symmetry of second partial derivatives. Conversely, given a collection \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) of non-vanishing vector fields which commute with each other ( \([X_i, X_j] = 0\) ) and are linearly independent at each point, one can always find a local chart for which they are precisely the coordinate basis vector fields.
