    Vectors

    1. Vectors in Curved Geometry

    In prerelativistic physics, it's assumed that space has natural structure of 3-dimensional vector space, once one has designated a point to serve as an origin.

    In special relativity, spacetime similarly has natural structure of 4-dimensional vector space.

    But, when one considers curved geometry, the vector space structure is lost. There is no natural notion how to add two points on a sphere and end up on the sphere?

    Nevertheless, the vector space structure can be recovered in the limit of infinitesimal displacements about a point.

    We would like to give a notion of tangent vector at \(p\) which only refers to intrinsic structure of a manifold, not to its embeddings in \(\mathbb{R}^n\).

    In \(\mathbb{R}^n\), a vector \(v = (v^1, \dots, v^n)\) defines the directional derivative operator \(\displaystyle \sum_\mu v^\mu \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu} \right)\), and vice versa.

    Directional derivatives are characterized by linearity and Leibnitz rule. On a manifold \(M\), let \(\mathcal{F}\) denote the collection of \(\mathcal{C}^\infty\) functions from \(M\) into \(\mathbb{R}\). We define a tangent vector \(v\) at \(p \in M\) to be a map \(v: \mathcal{F} \to \mathbb{R}\) which is:

    1. Linearity : \(v(af + bg) = a v(f) + b v(g) \quad \forall f,g \in \mathcal{F}, \ a,b \in \mathbb{R}\).
    2. Leibnitz rule : \(v(fg) = f(p)v(g) + g(p)v(f)\).

    If \(h \in \mathcal{F}\) is a constant function, i.e., \(h(q) = c \quad \forall q \in M\), then \(v(h) = 0\) because \(v(h^2) = 2cv(h)\), \(v(h^2) = v(ch) = cv(h)\).

    This definition make precise meaning to a concept of infinitesimal displacement.

    Let \(V_p\) be the collection of tangent vectors at \(p\). Then, \((v_1 + v_2)f = v_1(f) + v_2(f)\) and \((av)(f) = a v(f)\).

    Second property of \(V_p\) is given by the following theorem:

    Theorem: Let \(M\) be an n-dimensional manifold. Let \(p \in M\) and \(V_p\) denote tangent space at \(p\). Then, \(\dim V_p = n\).

    Proof. We shall show that \(\dim V_p = n\) by constructing a basis of \(V_p\), i.e., by finding \(n\) linearly independent tangent vectors which span \(V_p\).

    Let \(\psi: O \to U \subset \mathbb{R}^n\) be a chart with \(p \in O\). For \(\mu = 1, \dots, n\), define \(X_\mu: \mathcal{F} \to \mathbb{R}\) by:

    \[X_\mu(f) = \left. \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu} (f \circ \psi^{-1}) \right|_{\psi(p)}\]

    where \((x^1, \dots, x^n)\) are the Cartesian coordinates of \(\mathbb{R}^n\). It is easily seen that \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are linearly independent tangent vectors.

    To show that they span \(V_p\), we expand \(f \circ \psi^{-1}\) around \(a = \psi(p)\). For all \(q \in O\) we have:

    \[f(q) = f(p) + \sum_{\mu=1}^n [x^\mu \circ \psi(q) - x^\mu \circ \psi(p)] H_\mu(\psi(q))\]

    where the functions \(H_\mu\) satisfy \(H_\mu(\psi(p)) = X_\mu(f)\).

    Let \(v \in V_p\) be an arbitrary tangent vector. We apply \(v\) to \(f\), using linearity, the Leibnitz property, and the fact that \(v\) applied to a constant (like \(f(p)\) or \(x^\mu \circ \psi(p)\)) vanishes. Evaluating at \(p\), the terms where \(v\) acts on \(H_\mu\) zero out because the position difference is zero at \(p\). We obtain:

    \[v(f) = \sum_{\mu=1}^n [H_\mu \circ \psi(p)] v(x^\mu \circ \psi)\]

    Substituting \(H_\mu \circ \psi(p) = X_\mu(f)\), we get:

    \[v(f) = \sum_{\mu=1}^n v^\mu X_\mu(f)\]

    where the coefficients are defined as \(v^\mu = v(x^\mu \circ \psi)\).

    Thus, we have successfully expressed an arbitrary tangent vector \(v\) as a linear combination of the \(X_\mu\):

    \[v = \sum_{\mu=1}^n v^\mu X_\mu\]

    This shows the \(n\) vectors \(X_\mu\) span the tangent space, which completes the proof that \(\dim V_p = n\). \(\blacksquare\)

    The basis \(\{X_\mu \}\) of the proof is called a coordinate basis. We often denote \(X_\mu\) as \(\partial / \partial x^\mu\).

    2. Basis Transformation and Vector Transformation

    By choosing a different chart \(\psi'\), we obtain a different coordinate basis \(\{X_\mu '\}\). Since \(\{X_\mu '\}\) also spans \(V_p\), we can express \(X_\mu\) in terms of the new basis \(\{X_\mu '\}\). Using chain rule, we get $$ X_{\mu} = \sum_{\mu = 1}^n \left . \frac{\partial x' ^\nu}{\partial x^\mu} \right \vert {\psi (p)} X{\mu} ' $$ where \(x' ^\nu\) denotes the \(\nu\)th component of the map \(\psi ' \circ \psi^{-1}\). Hence, we find that components of \(v' ^\nu\) of a vector \(v\) in the new coordinate basis are related to the components \(v^\mu\) in the old basis by $$ v'^\nu = \sum_{\mu=1}^n v^\mu \frac{\partial x' ^\nu}{\partial x^\mu} . $$ This is called as the vector transformation law.

    3. Tangent of a Curve

    A smooth curve \(C\) on a manifold \(M\) is a \(\mathcal{C}^\infty\) map of \(\mathbb{R}\) into \(M\). At each point \(p\in M\) lying on the curve, we can associate a tangent vector \(T \in V_p\) with \(C\) as following. For \(f \in \mathcal{F}\), set \(T(f) = d (f \circ C)/dt\). Then, $$ T(f) = \frac{d}{dt} (f \circ C) = \sum_{\mu} \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu} (f \circ \psi^{-1}) \frac{dx^\mu}{dt} = \sum_{\mu} \frac{dx^\mu}{dt} X_{\mu} (f) $$ because the curve \(C\) on \(M\) will get mapped into a curve \(x^\mu (t)\) in \(\R\) when we choose a coordinate system \(\psi\).
