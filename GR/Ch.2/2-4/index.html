2.4 Abstract Index Notation

1. Why Abstract Index Notation?

Tensors of high type are functions of many vectors and dual vectors. If we operate with these tensors, the notation becomes extremely complicated. Also, a given tensor can be viewed in a variety of equivalent ways. To solve this problem, we have introduced a basis, so that we can characterize a tensor by its components \(T^{\mu_1 \cdots \mu_k}{}_{\nu_1 \cdots \nu_l}\) .

However, this component notation has a problem; if we do not specify how the basis we use is chosen, the equations we write will have a basis-independent meaning. However, in some cases, it will be convenient to use a particular type of basis, e.g., a coordinate basis adapted to the symmetries of a particular spacetime. If we do this, the equations may be valid only in this basis. It is important to make a clear distinction between equations that hold between tensors and equations for their components that hold only in a special basis. Using the component notation, this distinction is blurred.

So, we shall use a notation called the abstract index notation which is merely a slight modification of the component notation. The idea is not to introduce a basis but to use a notation for tensors that mirrors the expressions for their basis components.

2. Rules of Abstract Index Notation

The rules of the abstract index notation are as follows:

Remark (Rules of Abstract Index Notation).

  • A tensor of type \((k,l)\) will be denoted by a letter followed by \(k\) contravariant (upper) and \(l\) covariant (lower) lowercase Latin indices, e.g., \(T^{a_1 \cdots a_k}{}_{b_1 \cdots b_l}\) .
  • The Latin indices here should be viewed as reminders of the number and type of variables the tensor acts on, not as basis components.
  • Any lowercase Latin letters can be placed in any slot, but in any equation, the same letter must be used to represent the same slot on both sides of the equation.

For example, \(T^{abc}{}_{be}\) denotes the tensor of type \((2,1)\) , obtained by contracting \(T^{abc}{}_{de}\) with respect to the second contravariant and first covariant slots.

The outer product of two tensors is denoted by simply writing them adjacent to each other and omitting the \(\otimes\) sign. Thus, \(T^{abc}{}_{de} S^f{}_g\) denotes the tensor of type \((4,3)\) obtained by taking the outer product of \(T^{abc}{}_{de}\) and \(S^f{}_g\) .

Using the index notation, we can write down true tensor equations since no basis has been introduced. However, one might want to write an equation for the components of a tensor.

Remark (Component Notation Rule). Component labels in the component notation will be denoted with Greek letters.

For example, \(T^{\mu \nu \lambda}{}_{\sigma \rho}\) denotes a basis component of the abstract tensor \(T^{abc}{}_{de}\) .

3. Advantages

Given any tensor equation in the index notation, the corresponding equation (with Greek \(\longleftrightarrow\) Latin replacements) holds for the basis components in any basis. Conversely, for any equation relating basis components which is a true tensor equation, the corresponding tensor equation in the index notation is valid.

The main advantage of the index notation is that one is not forced to introduce a basis unnecessarily, and one is assured that all equations written in the index notation are equations holding globally between tensors.

4. Exceptional Rules for the Metric Tensor

Some additional rules apply to the metric tensor. Since a metric tensor is a tensor of type \((0,2)\) , it is denoted as \(g_{ab}\) .

If we apply the metric to a vector \(v^a\) , we get a dual vector \(g_{ab} v^b\) . It is convenient to denote this dual vector simply as \(v_a\) , thus making notationally explicit the isomorphism between \(V_p\) and \(V_p^*\) .

The inverse of \(g_{ab}\) is a tensor of type \((2,0)\) and could be denoted as \((g^{-1})^{ab}\) . However, we conventionally drop the inverse sign and write it as \(g^{ab}\) . By definition, they satisfy

\[ g^{ab} g_{bc} = \delta^a{}_c \]

If we apply the inverse metric to a dual vector \(\omega_a\) , we get a vector \(g^{ab} \omega_b\) , which we simply denote as \(\omega^a\) . In general, raised or lowered indices on any tensor denote the application of the metric or inverse metric to that specific slot.

For example, if \(T^{abc}{}_{de}\) is a tensor of type \((3,2)\) , then \(T^a{}_b{}^{cde}\) denotes the tensor obtained via

\[ T^a{}_b{}^{cde} = g_{bf} g^{dh} g^{ej} T^{afc}{}_{hj}. \]

5. Symmetry of Tensors

The abstract index notation may also be used to neatly express the symmetry properties of tensors.

A tensor \(T_{ab}\) of type \((0,2)\) takes a pair of vectors \((v^a, w^a)\) into a scalar number \(T_{ab} v^a w^b\) . We may also consider a new tensor evaluated as \(T_{ab} v^b w^a\) . In the index notation, this new tensor is intuitively denoted as \(T_{ba}\) . Thus, the equation \(T_{ab} = T_{ba}\) strictly says that the tensor \(T_{ab}\) is symmetric.

We can generally apply this rule to higher types of tensors. We might introduce a notation for the totally symmetric and totally antisymmetric parts of tensors.

Definition 2.4.1 (Symmetric & Antisymmetric Parts). If \(T_{ab}\) is a tensor of type \((0,2)\) , we define

\[T_{(ab)} = \frac{1}{2} \left( T_{ab} + T_{ba} \right)\]
\[T_{[ab]} = \frac{1}{2} \left( T_{ab} - T_{ba} \right)\]

as symmetric and antisymmetric parts of tensors.

More generally, for a tensor \(T_{a_1 \cdots a_l}\) of type \((0,l)\) , we define the totally symmetric and totally antisymmetric parts over all indices as

\[ T_{(a_1 \cdots a_l)} = \frac{1}{l!} \sum_{\pi} T_{a_{\pi(1)} \cdots a_{\pi(l)}} \]
\[ T_{[a_1 \cdots a_l]} = \frac{1}{l!} \sum_{\pi} \delta_{\pi} T_{a_{\pi(1)} \cdots a_{\pi(l)}} \]

where the sum is taken over all permutations \(\pi\) , and \(\delta_\pi\) is \(+1\) for even permutations and \(-1\) for odd permutations .

Similar definitions apply for any isolated group of bracketed covariant or contravariant indices. For example

\[ T^{(ab)c}{}_{[de]} = \frac{1}{4} \left[ T^{abc}{}_{de} + T^{bac}{}_{de} - T^{abc}{}_{ed} - T^{bac}{}_{ed} \right] \]

Definition 2.4.2 (Differential Form). A totally antisymmetric tensor field \(T_{a_1 \cdots a_l}\) of type \((0,l)\) satisfying

\[T_{a_1 \cdots a_l} = T_{[a_1 \cdots a_l]}\]

is called a differential \(l\) -form .

If one strictly deals with differential forms, it is sometimes convenient to drop the index notation entirely and denote an \(l\) -form simply in boldface, such as \(\mathbf{T}\) .
