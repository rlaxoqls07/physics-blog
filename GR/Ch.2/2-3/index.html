2.3 Tensors; the Metric Tensor

Many physical quantities have a linear (or multilinear) dependence on directional displacements. For example, consider the measurement of a magnetic field. For each oriented probe (a vector), a scalar number is recorded; but there is an infinite number of possible orientations. However, we only need to test three linearly independent probe directions to know the outcome for any direction. This leads to the fundamental mathematical fact that the magnetic field acts as a linear map from vectors to scalars—precisely the definition of a dual vector.

In this section, we rigorously define vectors, dual vectors, and other quantities called tensors , which exhibit a similar multilinear dependence on multiple vectors simultaneously.

1. Dual Space and Double Dual Space

Definition 2.3.1 (Dual Space). Let \(V\) be any finite-dimensional vector space over \(\mathbb{R}\) . Consider the collection \(V^*\) of all linear maps \(f : V \to \mathbb{R}\) . We call \(V^*\) the dual vector space of \(V\) , and elements of \(V^*\) are called dual vectors (or 1-forms).

If \(v_1, \dots, v_n\) is a basis of \(V\) , we can define specific elements \(v^{1*}, \dots, v^{n*} \in V^*\) by their selective action on the basis vectors: $$ v^{\mu } (v_{\nu}) = \delta^\mu_\nu $$ Then, it naturally follows that \(\{v^{\mu*}\}\) forms a basis for \(V^*\) , called the dual basis * to the basis \(\{v_\mu\}\) of \(V\) . This explicitly shows that \(\dim V = \dim V^*\) . We can naturally consider the correspondence between \(v_\mu\) and \(v^{\mu*}\) , which gives rise to an isomorphism between \(V\) and \(V^*\) .

However, it is crucial to note that this isomorphism strictly depends on the choice of basis \(\{v_\mu\}\) . Without additional structures (like a metric), there is no natural, basis-independent way of identifying \(V\) and \(V^*\) .

We can push this further and consider the double dual space \(V^{**}\) of \(V\) . A vector \(v^{**} \in V^{**}\) is a linear map from \(V^*\) into \(\mathbb{R}\) . Interestingly, \(V^{**}\) is naturally isomorphic to the original vector space \(V\) . For each vector \(v \in V\) , we can simply associate the map in \(V^{**}\) whose value on any \(\omega^* \in V^*\) is purely evaluation: \(\omega^*(v)\) .

So, taking a double dual yields nothing fundamentally new, and we conventionally identify \(V^{**}\) directly with the original vector space \(V\) .

2. Tensors

Definition 2.3.2 (Tensor). Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional vector space and let \(V^*\) denote its dual vector space. A tensor \(T\) of type \((k,l)\) over \(V\) is a multilinear map: $ \(T : \underbrace{V^* \times \dots \times V^*}_{k \text{ times}} \times \underbrace{V \times \dots \times V}_{l \text{ times}} \to \mathbb{R}\) $

For example: * A tensor of type \((0,1)\) takes one vector and maps it to \(\mathbb{R}\) —this is a dual vector . * A tensor of type \((1,0)\) takes one dual vector and maps it to \(\mathbb{R}\) —this is an element of \(V^{**}\) . However, since we identify \(V^{**}\) with \(V\) , a tensor of type \((1,0)\) is nothing more than an ordinary vector .

We define the collection \(\mathcal{T}(k,l)\) of all tensors of type \((k,l)\) , which possesses the structure of a vector space. Since there are \(n^{k+l}\) independent ways of filling the individual slots of a tensor of type \((k,l)\) using basis elements, the dimension of the vector space \(\mathcal{T}(k,l)\) is exactly \(n^{k+l}\) .

3. Contraction and Outer Product

We now introduce two fundamental operations on tensors. The first is called a contraction with respect to the \(i\) -th dual vector slot and \(j\) -th vector slot.

Definition 2.3.3 (Contraction). The contraction is a map \(C : \mathcal{T}(k,l) \to \mathcal{T}(k-1, l-1)\) defined as: $ \(CT = \sum_{\sigma = 1}^n T(\dots, v^{\sigma*}, \dots ; \dots, v_{\sigma}, \dots)\) $ where \(\{v_\sigma\}\) is a basis of \(V\) , \(\{v^{\sigma*}\}\) is its dual basis, and these are inserted into the \(i\) -th and \(j\) -th slots of \(T\) . (The resulting sum is independent of the choice of basis).

The next algebraic operation on tensors is the outer product.

Definition 2.3.4 (Outer Product). Given a tensor \(T\) of type \((k,l)\) and another tensor \(T'\) of type \((k', l')\) , we can construct a new tensor of type \((k+k', l+l')\) called the outer product of \(T\) and \(T'\) , denoted as \(T \otimes T'\) .

Given \((k+k')\) dual vectors \(v^{1*}, \dots, v^{(k+k')*}\) and \((l+l')\) vectors \(w_1, \dots, w_{l+l'}\) , we define the action of \(T \otimes T'\) on these inputs to be the scalar product: $ \((T \otimes T') = T(v^{1*}, \dots, v^{k*} ; w_1, \dots, w_l) \cdot T'(v^{(k+1)*}, \dots, v^{(k+k')*} ; w_{l+1}, \dots, w_{l+l'})\) $

One way of constructing general tensors is to take outer products of vectors and dual vectors. A tensor which can be expressed entirely as such a chained outer product is called simple . If \(\{v_\mu\}\) is a basis of \(V\) and \(\{v^{\mu*}\}\) is its dual basis, then the \(n^{k+l}\) simple tensors \(\{v_{\mu_1} \otimes \dots \otimes v_{\mu_k} \otimes v^{\nu_1*} \otimes \dots \otimes v^{\nu_l*}\}\) yield a complete basis for \(\mathcal{T}(k,l)\) .

Thus, every tensor \(T\) of type \((k,l)\) can be expanded in components as a sum of simple tensors in this collection: $$ T = \sum_{\mu, \nu = 1}^n T^{\mu_1 \dots \mu_k}{} {\nu_1 \dots \nu_l} \, v \otimes v^{\nu_1} \otimes \dots \otimes v_{\mu_k } \otimes \dots \otimes v^{\nu_l } $$ If \(T'\) has components \({T'}^{\mu_1' \dots \mu_{k'}'}{}_{\nu_1' \dots \nu_{l'}'}\) , then the outer product \(S = T \otimes T'\) simply has components given by the product: $$ S^{\mu_1 \dots \mu_{k+k'}}{} {\nu_1 \dots \nu }} = T^{\mu_1 \dots \mu_k}{ {\nu_1 \dots \nu_l} \; {T'}^{\mu_1' \dots \mu }'}{ {\nu_1' \dots \nu $$}'

4. Tensors on Tangent Spaces and Transformation Laws

So far, we've discussed tensors on an arbitrary finite-dimensional vector space \(V\) . In differential geometry, we consider \(V\) as the tangent space \(V_p\) at a point \(p\) on a manifold \(\mathcal{M}\) .

In this specific case: * The dual space \(V_p^*\) is usually called the cotangent space at \(p\) , and vectors in \(V_p^*\) are called cotangent vectors (or covariant vectors). * We also usually refer to standard vectors in \(V_p\) historically as contravariant vectors .

We can construct a local coordinate basis \(\{\partial / \partial x^1, \dots, \partial / \partial x^n\}\) for \(V_p\) . The canonically associated dual basis of \(V_p^*\) is denoted as \(\{dx^1, \dots, dx^n\}\) . They satisfy the strict duality condition \(dx^\mu(\partial / \partial x^\nu) = \delta^\mu_\nu\) .

We already showed in the previous section that under a coordinate transformation \(x \to x'\) , vector components transform as: $$ v'^{\mu'} = \sum_{\mu=1}^n v^\mu \frac{\partial x'^{\mu'}}{\partial x^\mu} $$ If \(\omega_\mu\) denotes the components of a dual vector \(\omega\) with respect to the dual basis \(\{dx^\mu\}\) , preserving the scalar contraction \(\omega(v)\) requires them to transform inversely: $$ \omega_{\mu'}' = \sum_{\mu=1}^n \omega_\mu \frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial x'^{\mu'}} $$ More generally, the components of a tensor in \(\mathcal{T}(k,l)\) transform by acquiring one Jacobian matrix factor for each index: $$ T^{\prime \mu_1^\prime \dots \mu_k^\prime}{} {\nu_1^\prime \dots \nu_l^\prime} = \sum \right) $$ This is universally referred to as the }^n T^{\mu_1 \dots \mu_k}{}_{\nu_1 \dots \nu_l} \left( \frac{\partial x^{\prime \mu_1^\prime}}{\partial x^{\mu_1}} \dots \right) \left( \dots \frac{\partial x^{\nu_l}}{\partial x^{\prime \nu_l^\prime} tensor transformation law .

5. Tensor Fields

An assignment of a tensor over \(V_p\) for each point \(p\) in the manifold \(\mathcal{M}\) is called a tensor field .

The smoothness of a contravariant vector field \(v\) was already defined earlier in the previous section . Building upon this, a covariant vector field \(\omega\) is defined to be smooth if, for every smooth vector field \(v\) , the resulting scalar function \(\omega(v)\) is smooth on the manifold.

Generalizing completely, a tensor field \(T\) of type \((k,l)\) is considered smooth if, for all possible smooth covariant vector fields \(\omega^1, \dots, \omega^k\) and all smooth contravariant vector fields \(v_1, \dots, v_l\) , the evaluation \(T(\omega^1, \dots, \omega^k ; v_1, \dots, v_l)\) yields a strictly smooth scalar function on \(\mathcal{M}\) .

6. The Metric Tensor

Definition 2.3.5 (Metric Tensor). A metric \(g\) is a linear map from \(V_p \times V_p\) into numbers; i.e., it is a tensor field of type \((0,2)\) . In addition, the metric is required to be symmetric and non-degenerate. * Symmetric: For all \(v_1, v_2 \in V_p\) , we have \(g(v_1, v_2) = g(v_2, v_1)\) . * Non-degenerate: \(g(v_1, v_2) = 0\) for all \(v_2\) if and only if \(v_1 = 0\) .

Thus, a metric \(g\) on a manifold \(\mathcal{M}\) is a symmetric, non-degenerate tensor field of type \((0,2)\) . In other words, a metric provides a (not necessarily positive-definite) inner product on the tangent space at each point.

In a coordinate basis, we may expand a metric \(g\) in terms of its matrix components \(g_{\mu \nu}\) : $$ g = \sum_{\mu, \nu} g_{\mu \nu} dx^\mu \otimes dx^\nu $$ In physical literature, we frequently use the line element notation \(ds^2\) to represent the metric tensor: $$ ds^2 = \sum_{\mu, \nu} g_{\mu \nu} dx^\mu dx^\nu $$ where the outer product sign ( \(\otimes\) ) is implicitly omitted for brevity.

Given a metric \(g\) , we can always find an orthonormal basis \(\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}\) for the tangent space at a point; i.e., \(g(v_\mu, v_\nu) = 0\) (if \(\mu \ne \nu\) ) and \(g(v_\mu, v_\mu) = \pm 1\) . By Sylvester's Law of Inertia, the number of basis vectors with \(+1\) and the number with \(-1\) are strictly independent of the choice of orthonormal basis. The ordered sequence of \(+\) and \(-\) signs occurring is called the signature of the metric.

Remark (Riemannian vs. Lorentzian Metrics). In standard differential geometry, we usually deal with purely positive-definite metrics (signature \(++++\) ). These spaces are called Riemannian . On the other hand, the metric of spacetime in General Relativity requires a signature of \(-+++\) (or \(+---\) ). Such manifolds, which possess one time-like dimension and the remainder space-like, are mathematically defined as Lorentzian manifolds.

As defined above, a metric \(g\) is fundamentally a tensor of type \((0,2)\) over \(V_p\) . However, we can also view \(g\) dynamically as a linear map from \(V_p\) into \(V_p^*\) defined by \(v \mapsto g(\cdot, v)\) . Because of the non-degeneracy condition, this map is strictly one-to-one and onto. Thus, we can universally use the metric \(g\) to establish a perfect, natural one-to-one correspondence between vectors and dual vectors (often referred to as "index raising and lowering").
