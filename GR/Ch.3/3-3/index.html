Part 3: Geodesics and Manifold Curvature

Continuing our rigorous progression through the foundations of General Relativity, we now turn our attention to geodesics. In flat space, straight lines are trivial, but on a general curved manifold, we must fundamentally redefine what it means for a curve to be "straight." By leveraging our previously defined derivative operator, we can construct coordinate systems uniquely adapted to the manifold's geometry and explicitly relate the behavior of these curves to the Riemann curvature tensor.

1. The Geodesic Equation

Intuitively, geodesics are lines that curve as little as possible—the straightest possible lines in a given geometry.

Definition 3.3.1 (Geodesic). Given a derivative operator \(\nabla_a\) , a geodesic is a curve whose tangent vector is parallel propagated along itself. That is, a curve whose tangent \(T^a\) satisfies:

\[T^a \nabla_{a} T^b = 0\]

To satisfy the purely geometric requirement that the curve be as straight as possible, we technically only require that the tangent vector point in the same direction as itself when parallel propagated. This yields a weaker condition:

\[T^a \nabla_a T^b = \alpha T^b\]

where \(\alpha\) is an arbitrary function along the curve. However, we can always reparameterize the curve to eliminate \(\alpha\) and recover the strict zero condition. A parameterization that satisfies \(T^a \nabla_a T^b = 0\) is called an affine parameterization .

Let \(\psi\) be a coordinate system mapping the geodesic into a curve \(x^\mu (t)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) . The components \(T^\mu\) of \(T^a\) must satisfy:

\[\frac{dT^\mu}{dt} + \sum_{\sigma , \nu} \Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\sigma \nu} T^\sigma T^\nu = 0\]

Substituting the explicit definition of the tangent vector \(T^\mu = dx^\mu / dt\) , we obtain the geodesic equation in a coordinate basis:

\[\frac{d^2 x^\mu}{dt^2} + \sum_{\sigma , \nu} \Gamma^\mu{}_{\sigma \nu} \frac{dx^\sigma}{dt} \frac{dx^\nu}{dt} = 0\]

This represents a coupled system of \(n\) second-order ordinary differential equations. By standard existence and uniqueness theorems for ODEs, this system always has a unique solution for any given initial position \(x^\mu\) and initial velocity \(dx^\mu / dt\) . Thus, given any point \(p \in \mathcal{M}\) and any initial tangent vector \(T^a \in V_p\) , there exists a unique geodesic through \(p\) with tangent \(T^a\) .

2. Exponential Map and Riemannian Normal Coordinates

The existence and uniqueness of geodesics provide a powerful tool for constructing coordinate systems that drastically simplify local computations.

Definition 3.3.2 (Exponential Map). Let \(p \in \mathcal{M}\) . The exponential map is a map from the tangent space \(V_p\) to the manifold \(\mathcal{M}\) . It maps a tangent vector \(T^a \in V_p\) to the point in \(\mathcal{M}\) that lies at a unit affine parameter ( \(t=1\) ) from \(p\) along the unique geodesic passing through \(p\) with initial tangent \(T^a\) .

While geodesics may eventually cross or encounter singularities, there always exists a sufficiently small neighborhood of the origin in \(V_p\) where the exponential map is strictly well-defined and one-to-one.

Because \(V_p\) is an \(n\) -dimensional vector space, it is naturally isomorphic to \(\mathbb{R}^n\) . By using the exponential map to project the Cartesian coordinates of the tangent space directly onto the manifold \(\mathcal{M}\) , we construct Riemannian normal coordinates at \(p\) .

This coordinate system has two defining structural advantages: * All geodesics passing through \(p\) are mapped into perfectly straight lines passing through the origin of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) . * The connection coefficients (Christoffel symbols), \(\Gamma^\mu_{\sigma\nu}\) , strictly vanish at the point \(p\) , vastly simplifying local tensor calculus.

3. Gaussian Normal Coordinates (Synchronous Coordinates)

While Riemannian normal coordinates provide a local flattening around a single point \(p\) , we often need coordinate systems adapted to the time evolution of spacetime, such as in cosmological models or the \((3+1)\) ADM decomposition.

When our calculations involve an \((n-1)\) -dimensional embedded submanifold (a hypersurface \(S\) ), we utilize Gaussian normal coordinates (or synchronous coordinates).

At each point \(p \in S\) , the tangent space of the hypersurface can be viewed as an \((n-1)\) -dimensional subspace of the full tangent space \(V_p\) of \(\mathcal{M}\) . Consequently, there exists a unique normal vector \(n^a \in V_p\) that is strictly orthogonal to all vectors in the tangent space of \(S\) (with respect to the metric \(g_{ab}\) ). If \(S\) is a non-null hypersurface, we normalize \(n^a\) such that \(g_{ab}n^a n^b = \pm 1\) .

We construct Gaussian normal coordinates as follows:

  1. For every point \(p \in S\) , construct the unique geodesic through \(p\) with the initial tangent vector \(n^a\) .
  2. Choose arbitrary coordinates \((x^1, \dots, x^{n-1})\) on a domain of the hypersurface \(S\) .
  3. Label each point in the neighborhood of \(S\) by the affine parameter \(t\) along its corresponding geodesic , and the coordinates \((x^1, \dots, x^{n-1})\) of its origin point \(p \in S\) .

This yields the coordinate chart \(q \mapsto (x^1, \dots, x^{n-1}, t)\) . A crucial geometric property of Gaussian normal coordinates is that the geodesics remain orthogonal to all hypersurfaces \(S_t\) defined by \(t = \text{Const}\) .

Proof of Preserved Orthogonality. By construction, orthogonality holds on \(S_0 = S\) . We must prove that the geodesic tangent field \(n^a\) remains orthogonal to the coordinate basis fields \(X_1^a, \dots, X_{n-1}^a\) generating the tangent space of \(S_t\) everywhere. Denoting any one of these fields as \(X^a\) :

\[\begin{aligned} n^b \nabla_b \left(n_a X^a \right) &= n_a n^b \nabla_b X^a \\ &= n_a X^b \nabla_b n^a \\ &= \frac{1}{2} X^b \nabla_b \left(n^a n_a \right) \\ &= 0 \end{aligned}\]

The first equality applies the geodesic equation ( \(n^b \nabla_b n_a = 0\) ). The second equality relies on the fact that \(n^a\) and \(X^b\) , as elements of a coordinate basis, commute ( \([n, X]=0 \implies n^b\nabla_b X^a = X^b\nabla_b n^a\) ). The third uses the Leibniz rule. The final equality holds because the normalization \(n^a n_a = \pm 1\) is constant on \(\mathcal{M}\) . Since \(n_a X^a = 0\) initially on \(S\) , its derivative along the geodesic is zero, proving it remains zero everywhere. \(\blacksquare\)

4. Variational Principles: Extremizing Length and Proper Time

A profound property of geodesics derived from a metric-compatible connection is that they extremize the length (or proper time ) of curves connecting two points.

For a smooth curve \(C\) with a Riemannian metric \(g_{ab}\) , the length \(l\) is:

\[l = \int_a^b \left( g_{ab} T^a T^b \right)^{1/2} dt\]

where \(T^a\) is the tangent to \(C\) . Length is invariant under reparameterization \(s = s(t)\) :

\[l' = \int_a^b \left[ g_{ab} S^a S^b \right]^{1/2} ds = \int_a^b \left[ g_{ab} T^a T^b \right]^{1/2} \frac{dt}{ds} ds = l\]

For a metric of Lorentz signature \((-++\cdots+)\) , we classify curves by the norm of their tangent:

  • Timelike : \(g_{ab} T^a T^b < 0\)
  • Null : \(g_{ab} T^a T^b = 0\)
  • Spacelike : \(g_{ab} T^a T^b > 0\)

For timelike curves, we extremize the proper time \(\tau\) :

\[\tau = \int_a^b \left( -g_{ab} T^a T^b \right)^{1/2} dt\]

We wish to derive the condition that extremizes this functional. Focusing on spacelike curves (the timelike derivation is identical up to a sign), we treat this as a Lagrangian mechanics action problem. Because length is parameter-independent, we choose an affine parameterization such that \(g_{ab} T^a T^b = 1\) . The variation \(\delta l\) is:

\[\begin{aligned} 0 &= \int_a^b \sum_{\alpha, \beta} \left\{ g_{\alpha \beta} \frac{dx^\alpha}{dt} \frac{d(\delta x^\beta)}{dt} + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\sigma} \frac{\partial g_{\alpha \beta}}{\partial x^\sigma} \frac{dx^\alpha}{dt} \frac{dx^\beta}{dt} \delta x^\sigma \right\} dt \\ &= \int_a^b \sum_{\alpha, \beta} \left\{ - \frac{d}{dt} \left( g_{\alpha \beta} \frac{dx^\alpha}{dt} \right) + \frac{1}{2} \sum_\lambda \frac{\partial g_{\alpha \lambda}}{\partial x^\beta} \frac{dx^\alpha}{dt} \frac{dx^\lambda}{dt} \right\} \delta x^\beta dt \end{aligned}\]

For this integral to vanish for arbitrary variations \(\delta x^\beta\) , the integrand must evaluate to zero:

\[- \sum_\alpha g_{\alpha \beta} \frac{d^2 x^\alpha}{dt^2} - \sum_{\alpha, \lambda} \frac{\partial g_{\alpha \beta}}{\partial x^\lambda} \frac{dx^\lambda}{dt} \frac{dx^\alpha}{dt} + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\alpha, \lambda} \frac{\partial g_{\alpha \lambda}}{\partial x^\beta} \frac{dx^\alpha}{dt} \frac{dx^\lambda}{dt} = 0\]

By isolating the second derivative and comparing it to the standard definition of the Christoffel symbols, this perfectly reproduces the geodesic equation. Thus, a curve extremizes length (or proper time ) if and only if it is a geodesic .

(Note: While geodesics extremize length, they are not guaranteed to be the global minimum. Two points on a sphere's equator can be connected by two distinct geodesics, only one of which represents the shortest path.)

5. The Geodesic Deviation Equation

Finally, we establish the physical manifestation of curvature by analyzing how families of geodesics behave relative to one another.

Let \(\gamma_s (t)\) denote a smooth one-parameter family of geodesics. For each \(s \in \mathbb{R}\) , \(\gamma_s\) is a geodesic parameterized by affine parameter \(t\) . The mapping \((t,s) \mapsto \gamma_s (t)\) defines a two-dimensional submanifold \(\Sigma\) . Because these are geodesics, their tangent vectors \(T^a = (\partial/\partial t)^a\) satisfy \(T^a \nabla_a T^b = 0\) .

We define the deviation vector \(X^a = (\partial / \partial s)^a\) , which represents the spatial displacement to an infinitesimally nearby geodesic . Because \(X^a\) and \(T^a\) are coordinate basis vectors of \(\Sigma\) , they commute:

\[T^b \nabla_b X^a = X^b \nabla_b T^a\]

By utilizing gauge freedom in the affine parameterization, we can enforce \(X^a T_a = 0\) everywhere on \(\Sigma\) .

The quantity \(v^a = T^b \nabla_b X^a\) describes the relative velocity of the nearby geodesic . Taking one more derivative gives the relative acceleration \(a^a = T^c \nabla_c v^a\) . We can directly relate this acceleration to the Riemann tensor:

\[\begin{aligned} a^a &= T^c \nabla_c (T^b \nabla_b X^a) \\\\ &= T^c \nabla_c (X^b \nabla_b T^a) \\\\ &= (T^c \nabla_c X^b)(\nabla_b T^a) + X^b T^c \nabla_c \nabla_b T^a \\\\ &= (X^c \nabla_c T^b)(\nabla_b T^a) + X^b T^c \nabla_b \nabla_c T^a - R_{cbd}{}^a X^b T^c T^d \\\\ &= X^c \nabla_c (T^b \nabla_b T^a) - R_{cbd}{}^a X^b T^c T^d \\\\ &= - R_{cbd}{}^a X^b T^c T^d \end{aligned}\]

This result is the celebrated geodesic deviation equation . It definitively proves that \(a^a = 0\) for all families of geodesics if and only if the Riemann curvature tensor vanishes ( \(R_{abc}{}^d = 0\) ). In other words, tidal forces—the convergence or divergence of initially parallel free-falling test particles—are the strict geometric consequence of a non-zero Riemann tensor.
