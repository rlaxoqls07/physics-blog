3.2 Curvature

In flat spaces, parallel transport is trivial: a vector remains exactly the same no matter what path you take from \(p\) to \(q\) . However, in a general manifold, parallel transport is intrinsically path-dependent. If we take a vector at \(p\) and parallel transport it around a closed loop, it will not necessarily return to its original orientation. This phenomenon—often called holonomy in differential geometry—is the purest geometric manifestation of curvature.

In this article, we will mathematically formalize this path dependency.

1. The Riemann Curvature Tensor

How do we measure path dependency locally, without having to integrate around macroscopic loops? We look at the failure of derivatives to commute.

If you are familiar with gauge theories in Quantum Field Theory, this concept is identical: the commutator of gauge covariant derivatives yields the field strength tensor \(F_{\mu\nu}\) . In General Relativity, the commutator of our spacetime covariant derivatives will yield the curvature of spacetime itself.

Let \(\nabla_a\) be a derivative operator. Let \(\omega_a\) be a dual vector field and \(f\) be a smooth scalar function. If we apply two successive covariant derivatives to \(f\omega_a\) , we get:

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_a \nabla_b \left ( f \omega_c \right ) &= \nabla_a \left ( \omega_c \nabla_b f + f \nabla_b \omega_c \right ) \\ &= \left ( \nabla_a \nabla_b f \right ) \omega_c + \nabla_b f \nabla_a \omega_c + \nabla_a f \nabla_b \omega_c + f \nabla_a \nabla_b \omega_c. \end{aligned} \]

Reversing the order of differentiation to find \(\nabla_b \nabla_a \left ( f \omega_c \right )\) and subtracting the two yields the commutator:

\[ \left ( \nabla_a \nabla_b - \nabla_b \nabla_a \right ) \left ( f \omega_c \right ) = f \left ( \nabla_a \nabla_b - \nabla_b \nabla_a \right ) \omega_c. \]

Notice something remarkable here: all the derivatives acting on \(f\) have perfectly canceled out. The resulting operator \(\left ( \nabla_a \nabla_b - \nabla_b \nabla_a \right )\) acting on \(\omega_c\) depends only on the value of \(\omega_c\) at that specific point \(p\) , not on its derivatives. Mathematically, this means the commutator acts purely algebraically, defining a linear map from dual vectors to type \((0,3)\) tensors.

Definition 3.2.1 (Riemann Curvature Tensor). Because the commutator acts as a purely local linear map, there must exist a unique tensor field \(R_{abc}{}^{d}\) of type \((1,3)\) such that for any dual vector field \(\omega_c\) :

\[ \nabla_a \nabla_b \omega_c - \nabla_b \nabla_a \omega_c = R_{abc} {}^d \omega_d. \tag{*} \]

This tensor \(R_{abc} {}^d\) is the Riemann curvature tensor , encoding all intrinsic curvature of the manifold.

2. Quantifying Path Dependency (Holonomy)

To prove that the Riemann tensor actually governs the macroscopic holonomy (the rotation of a vector transported around a loop), let's construct a small closed loop at \(p \in \mathcal{M}\) .

Choose a \(2\) -dimensional surface \(S\) through \(p\) with coordinates \(t\) and \(s\) . We form a loop by moving \(\Delta t\) along the \(s=0\) curve, moving \(\Delta s\) along the \(t= \Delta t\) curve, and then tracing our steps back.

Let \(v^a\) be an arbitrary vector at \(p\) . We want to compute the total change \(\delta (v^a \omega_a)\) after parallel transporting \(v^a\) around this loop. Let \(T^b\) and \(S^c\) be the coordinate tangent vectors to the \(t\) and \(s\) curves, respectively.

For the first leg (moving by \(\Delta t\) ), the change \(\delta_1\) evaluated at the midpoint is:

\[ \delta_1 = \left . \Delta t \frac{\partial}{\partial t} (v^a \omega_a ) \right \vert _{(\Delta t/2, 0)} = \left . \Delta t v^a T^b \nabla_b \omega_a \right \vert_{(\Delta t / 2 , 0)} \]

where we used the parallel transport condition \(T^b \nabla_b v^a = 0\) . When we combine the forward leg ( \(\delta_1\) ) and the backward leg ( \(\delta_3\) ), we get:

\[ \delta_{1} + \delta_3 = \Delta t \left \{ \left . v^a T^b \nabla_b \omega_a \right \vert_{(\Delta t / 2 , 0)} - \left . v^a T^b \nabla_b \omega_a \right \vert_{(\Delta t / 2 , \Delta s)} \right \} \]

As \(\Delta s \to 0\) , this difference vanishes. This tells us a crucial geometric fact: parallel transport is strictly path-independent at the first-order approximation . Curvature is fundamentally a second-order effect.

To capture the second-order effect, we evaluate how the quantity \(T^b \nabla_b \omega_a\) shifts as we move along \(s\) by \(\Delta s\) . This shift is dictated by the derivative along \(S^c\) . Taking care to parallel transport the vectors properly, the combined change becomes:

\[ \delta_1 + \delta_3 = - \Delta t \Delta s v^a S^c \nabla_c (T^b \nabla_b \omega_a) \]

Adding the equivalent contributions from the other two legs ( \(\delta_2\) and \(\delta_4\) ), the total accumulated change around the entire loop is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \delta (v^a \omega_a ) &= \Delta t \Delta s v^a \left \{T^c \nabla_c (S^b \nabla_b \omega_a) - S^c \nabla_c (T^b \nabla_b \omega_a ) \right \} \\ &= \Delta t \Delta s v^a T^c S^b (\nabla_c \nabla_b - \nabla_b \nabla_c) \omega_a \\ &= \Delta t \Delta s v^a T^c S^b R_{cba}{}^{d} \omega_d \end{aligned} \]

(Note that we used the fact that coordinate bases commute, \([T, S] = 0\) , to combine the derivatives). Stripping away the arbitrary dual vector \(\omega_a\) , the actual physical vector \(v^a\) experiences a shift of:

\[ \delta v^a = \Delta t \Delta s v^d T^c S^b R_{cbd}{}^a. \]

This elegantly confirms our intuition: the Riemann tensor is exactly the machine that takes in the area of a loop ( \(T^c S^b\) ) and an initial vector ( \(v^d\) ), and spits out the discrepancy ( \(\delta v^a\) ) caused by traversing that loop.

3. Algebraic Properties of the Riemann Tensor

Using equation \((*)\) , we can generalize the commutator action to arbitrary tensor fields. For instance, applying it to a vector field \(t^c\) yields:

\[ \left ( \nabla_a \nabla_b - \nabla_b \nabla_a \right ) t^c = - R_{abd}{}^c t^d. \]

By induction, this pattern extends to any tensor \(T^{c_1 \cdots c_k}{}_{d_1 \cdots d_l}\) by adding one Riemann term for every upper index and subtracting one for every lower index:

\[ \left ( \nabla_a \nabla_b - \nabla_b \nabla_a \right ) T^{c_1 \cdots c_k}_{d_1 \cdots d_l} = - \sum_{i=1}^{k} R_{abe}{}^{c_i} T^{c_1 \cdots e \cdots c_k}{}_{d_1 \cdots d_l} + \sum_{j=1}^{l} R_{abd_j} {}^{e} T^{c_1 \cdots c_k}{}_{d_1 \cdots e \cdots d_l}. \]

The Riemann tensor possesses several strict symmetries, greatly reducing its number of independent components:

  1. Antisymmetry in the first pair: \(R_{abc}{}^d = - R_{bac}{}^d\)

  2. Cyclic identity: \(R_{[abc]}{}^{d} = 0\)

  3. For a metric-compatible derivative ( \(\nabla_a g_{bc} = 0\) ), raising/lowering indices reveals antisymmetry in the second pair:

\[ R_{abcd} = - R_{abdc}. \]
  1. The Bianchi identity , which acts as the geometric equivalent of the homogeneous Maxwell equations ( \(dF = 0\) ), dictates that the differential behavior of curvature is highly constrained:
\[ \nabla_{[a} R_{bc]d}{}^e = \frac{1}{6} \left( \nabla_a R_{bcd}{}^e + \nabla_b R_{cad}{}^e + \nabla_c R_{abd}{}^e - \nabla_a R_{cbd}{}^e - \nabla_b R_{acd}{}^e - \nabla_c R_{bad}{}^e \right) = 0. \]

4. Decomposing Curvature: Trace and Trace-Free Parts

From a group theory perspective, we often want to break tensors down into their irreducible representations. For the Riemann tensor, this means separating it into its trace and trace-free components.

Because of its antisymmetry, taking the trace over the first two or last two indices yields zero. However, tracing over the first and third indices extracts a highly non-trivial symmetric tensor.

Definition 3.2.2 (Ricci Tensor and Scalar Curvature). The trace of the Riemann tensor over its first and third indices defines the Ricci tensor \(R_{ac}\) :

\[ R_{ac} = R_{abc}{}^{b}. \]

Taking the trace one step further yields a single invariant number, the scalar curvature \(R\) :

\[ R = R_{a}{}^a. \]

Physically, the Ricci tensor measures how volumes deform as they move through curved space. Due to the block symmetries of the Riemann tensor ( \(R_{abcd} = R_{cdab}\) ), the Ricci tensor is strictly symmetric: \(R_{ac} = R_{ca}\) .

What about the "leftover" curvature that doesn't change volume, but rather shears and stretches it (like tidal forces)? That is captured by the trace-free part.

Definition 3.2.3 (Weyl Tensor). For manifolds of dimension \(n \ge 3\) , the completely trace-free part of the curvature is the Weyl tensor , \(C_{abcd}\) , defined by:

\[ R_{abcd} = C_{abcd} + \frac{2}{n-2} \left ( g_{a[c}R_{d]b} - g_{b[c}R_{d]a} \right ) - \frac{2}{(n-1)(n-2)} R g_{a[c} g_{d]b}. \]

The Weyl tensor inherits all the algebraic symmetries (1, 2, and 3) of the Riemann tensor but has exactly zero trace in any pair of indices. Most profoundly, the Weyl tensor is entirely invariant under conformal transformations ( \(g_{ab} \to \Omega^2 g_{ab}\) ), which is why it is often referred to as the conformal tensor .

5. The Einstein Tensor

The Bianchi identity isn't just a geometric curiosity; it is the mathematical foundation for Einstein's field equations. If we contract the Bianchi identity twice, we find a profound conservation law.

First, contract over \(a\) and \(e\) :

\[ \nabla_a R_{bcd}{}^a + \nabla_b R_{cd} - \nabla_c R_{bd} = 0. \]

Next, raise the index \(d\) with the inverse metric and contract over \(b\) and \(d\) . The result simplifies beautifully to:

\[ \nabla_a R_{c}{}^a + \nabla_b R_c {}^b - \nabla_c R = 0 \]

which can be factored into a strictly divergence-free expression:

\[ \nabla^a G_{ab} = 0 \]

Definition 3.2.4 (Einstein Tensor). The geometrically conserved quantity derived from the contracted Bianchi identity is the Einstein tensor , defined as:

\[ G_{ab} = R_{ab} - \frac{1}{2} R g_{ab}. \]

Because \(\nabla^a G_{ab} = 0\) is a strict mathematical identity, \(G_{ab}\) is the perfect geometric counterpart to couple to the stress-energy tensor \(T_{ab}\) , which is also conserved ( \(\nabla^a T_{ab} = 0\) ) due to Noether's theorem and translation symmetry.
